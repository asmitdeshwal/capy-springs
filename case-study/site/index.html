01The problem

Interfaces that have to create the motivation

Most of my work is interfaces where the user already knows what they want — they came to book something, buy something, file something. I wanted a project where the interface has to create the motivation, not just serve it.

Casual mobile games are the hardest version of that problem. The player gives you six seconds before deciding whether to keep going. They play one-handed, on a bus, with the sound off. There is no onboarding budget, because nobody reads a tutorial. And every competitor in the genre has spent millions optimising the same loop.

I also wanted to test a second thing: whether I could take a project all the way to a working artefact by designing it precisely enough to be built, rather than handing over a Figma file and hoping.

02The brief I set myself

Six constraints, fixed before anything was designed

I wrote these down first and never moved them. They did more design work than any moodboard.

One thumb, no buttonsThe entire play loop is walking. Every station reacts to proximity. Taps exist only for UI, never for the loop. If the game needs a button, the design is wrong.
No failure state, everCozy and stressful cannot coexist. Nothing is ever taken away. A guest who runs out of patience simply walks off — no red, no penalty screen.
Fully offlineNo account, no server, no ads, no timers ticking while you are away to guilt you back. It runs from a file.
Readable at arm’s lengthMinimum 4 px drawn features, minimum 56 px tap targets, one call-to-action colour, and no state communicated by colour alone.
60 fps on a mid-range phonePerformance as a design constraint, not an engineering afterthought. A 12 ms frame budget shaped the art direction more than any style reference.
Not the genre templateIf a mechanic is standard in the genre, replace it with one that carries the theme.
03Positioning

Four pitches, judged on one question

I wrote four complete one-page concepts and judged them on: what is the thing you do with your thumb, and is it interesting on its own? Capy Springs won because its answer was not “collect and deposit” — it was lead. That last constraint did the rest of the work.

04Tempo

The loop is not the design. The tempo is.

The loop reads LEAD → SOAK → COLLECT → LIGHT, with two optional detours that multiply income. What makes it work is the clock: a cable car docks every 18 seconds at the start, stretching to 25 as the cars grow. That bell is the heartbeat, and everything is tuned around it.

Soaks last 8 seconds dropping to 5, with seats freeing in the order guests entered, so someone is always climbing in or out. The combo window is 2 seconds and is shared across every bath — three guests landing inside it is a chain, and splitting a full line across two decks to make five is the skill nobody is taught and everybody discovers.

Nothing on screen waits more than a second to react. Guests join 0.12 s apart. Coins reach the counter in 0.35 s. The upgrade sheet slides in over 0.2 s.

The decision the player actually makes, every twenty seconds, is which detour fits before the next car. That question is the whole game.

05First run

The first 30 seconds are a script, not a tutorial

I refused to ship a tutorial. Instead I wrote the opening as a second-by-second script — what is on screen, what the player does, what they hear — and built the systems to satisfy it.

0.0 sCream fades to the deck. The first cable car is already docked. Nothing has to be waited for.
0.6 sThree capybaras hop out with a speech bubble holding one icon: a bath. The arrow above Kit points at the nearest and says LEAD.
2.0 sFirst touch. The bell rings — that gesture is also what unlocks audio, so the player’s first input is confirmed by the world rather than by a sound effect. The joystick blooms under the thumb.
6.0 sFirst SPLASH x3: gold number, screen bump, arpeggio, one 40 ms freeze. Under six seconds to the first reward.
11.0 sGuests climb out and flick coins into a tray that sits on the path back to the platform. The player learns collecting by walking where they were already going.
19.0 sThey stand on an offering step. Coins toss into the box with rising pitch, the flame flares, paper tears off the second bath. First unlock, nineteen seconds in.
31.0 sThe loop is running. No text has appeared except four single words.

Three concessions live in here and nowhere else: the first car’s guests have infinite patience, their soak is shortened to 5 seconds, and if nothing has been touched by 3.1 seconds a ghost thumb loops a drag gesture until it is. That is the entire tutorial system.

06The hard part

One arrow, twelve rules

The hardest UX problem in a game like this is not teaching the mechanics. It is answering, at every single moment, “what now?” — without a quest log, a checklist, or a wall of badges.

My answer is one arrow above the player’s head, driven by a strict priority list evaluated every frame. First match wins.

  1. SOAK (chain live) → nearest warm bath with a free seat
  2. SOAK → the bath that seats the most of your line
  3. STOKE (urgent) → the boiler, only if heat is under 40
  4. YUZU → nearest bath that is not already golden
  5. STOKE → the woodpile
  6. LIGHT → the cheapest unlock you can afford
  7. LEAD → the nearest waiting guest
  8. COLLECT → the fullest coin tray within 400 px
  9. YUZU → a ripe tree, if a guest is waiting on food
  10. TAP → the station holding the cheapest upgrade
  11. YUZU → a ripe tree
  12. dim → the cheapest thing you are saving toward

Three rules make this an interface rather than a nag. It is subtle while you move and grows only when you stop — 70% opacity in motion, full size and bouncing after two seconds idle. It never points at nothing: rule 12 is a dim arrow at whatever you are saving for, and the arrow only disappears once everything is maxed, which is also how the game says “you have seen it all”. And words appear twice, then never again — seven one-word prompts, each shown the first two times its rule fires, never more than one on screen. After about two minutes the game goes quiet and the player is fluent.

This list is also the reason the game could be tested automatically — which turned out to matter more than I expected.

07UI system

Every control specified like a token

Everything is drawn on one canvas — no DOM, no component library. So each control had to be written down as precisely as a design system token.

The thumb zone is sacred. The camera is clamped so the arrival platform never sits below 65% of screen height. The bottom third belongs to the hand.

The joystick floats and trails. It appears wherever the thumb lands, has an 8 px dead zone, and reaches full speed at 70% of its radius so short travel is enough. Once the thumb passes 90 px from the base, the base follows the thumb — so reversing direction never needs a long swipe back. That one change is what made the game feel right on a phone rather than on a desktop with a mouse.

08Craft

Art direction written as rules a machine could follow

There are no asset files. Every shape is generated at runtime from the same primitives, which forced the art direction to become the most rigorous style guide I have ever had to produce.

Every object is two or three flat fills: a top colour, an 8–12 px darker “thickness” band along its bottom edge, and a soft ellipse shadow. That is the entire illusion of depth — no projection, no isometric maths. Personality lives in motion, not faces: squash-and-stretch on every landing, a tail that lags opposite to velocity, soaking guests who sink 4 px and puff a heart every three seconds.

The gate for any new character is the silhouette test: rendered as a flat ink shape at half scale, it must still be identifiable. Kit is ears and a tail. A capybara is a loaf with a snout. A duck is a ball with a beak.

Colour has grammar, not just tokens — three rules I never broke:

CTA vermilion#E4572ECalls to action, and nothing else
Amber#FFC857Money, fruit and glow. No character wears it
Fox#F08A3EThe only saturated red-orange on screen, so you find yourself instantly
Bath#2FA6A0Hot water. Cold water also gets a frost hatch, never just a colour change

Sound is UI. All 26 effects are synthesised — no files ship — and several carry information the screen does not. Guests joining play a blip rising in pitch with their position in the line, so you hear the line fill without looking. Coins climb a semitone each within a burst, so a big payout sounds big. An offering step ticks upward as it drains, so you know how close the unlock is without reading the arc. Nothing plays before the first touch — and that touch rings the cable car’s bell, so the browser’s audio unlock is disguised as an event in the world.

09Method

I simulated the game instead of guessing

Here is the problem with designing something like this: the mechanics interact. Heat drains faster while baths are occupied. Occupancy depends on how long your line is. Line length depends on what you unlocked. Unlocks depend on income. Income depends on chains. Chains depend on whether the arrow sent you to the right bath.

You cannot hold that in your head, and you will not find its failure modes by playing for ten minutes.

So I specified a headless simulator as a first-class deliverable, alongside the design document. It runs the real game — every system, every number — with no screen, driven by a bot that plays using the exact same priority list that draws the on-screen arrow. Thirty minutes of play, 108,000 frames, in about three seconds.

It checks invariants once per simulated second: no guest in two seats, nothing stuck in the line, the camera never inverting on a tall phone, the save round-tripping byte for byte, and the drawing layer never touching the seeded random number generator. It checks progression checkpoints. It records the economy minute by minute. A fuzz mode plays with random input for five minutes and simulates the phone being locked and unlocked with the clock shifted by random hours.

Why this is a design tool and not a QA tool: the bot plays with my arrow logic. So when the bot plays badly, my guidance design is what is broken — and I can see it in seconds instead of after twenty minutes of confused play.

10Findings

Two design failures it caught

The unlocks nobody was ever pointed at

In my original list, LIGHT — spend coins on the next unlock — sat below LEAD and COLLECT. Obviously correct on paper: guests are time-sensitive, money is not.

In simulation it was catastrophic. Once cars arrived every 21 seconds with five guests each, there was always someone waiting, so LEAD matched forever and the arrow never pointed at an unlock again after the first one. A player following it faithfully would grind indefinitely and never see the game open up.

The fix was to move LIGHT above both — justified by a number I only had because I had measured it. Lighting takes 1–2.5 seconds; platform patience is 40. The detour is free. It looked like a priority problem and was actually a timing problem.

The infinite firewood shuttle

Rule 3 said: if you are carrying logs and the boiler is not full, go to the boiler. Also obviously correct.

The simulator produced a player who walked between the woodpile and the boiler forever — passing the woodpile picked up logs, carrying logs triggered rule 3, delivering them routed back past the woodpile. The run ended with 158 chained Steam Rushes and 78% of guests lost: the most profitable per-guest state in the game, reached by abandoning every guest.

The fix was to gate the rule on actual need — logs are urgent only when heat is under 40 or the water has gone cold. Otherwise they ride along and get dropped off after the next collection.

Both would have presented to a human playtester as bad feel, not broken logic. The first feels like “progression is too slow, raise the payouts.” The second feels like “the boiler is annoying, slow the drain.” Both diagnoses are wrong, and both would have cost days of tuning numbers that were already correct. The simulator turned a feel problem into a logic problem I could read in a diff.

11Evidence

Then it told me not to touch the economy

I had derived the income curve by hand. When I ran the simulator against it, the shape was right and the magnitude was wrong — my table had not accounted for how much the player spends on upgrades along the way. So I replaced my table with the measured one.

The decision that came out of it was a restraint decision: I changed nothing about the payouts. The pacing the curve produced — first unlock at 12 seconds, a helper hired at 2:35, the third bath at 5:06, the finale around 25 minutes — matched what I had designed on paper. The number that was wrong was my arithmetic, not the game.

To keep that honest, the simulator now prints a balance warning if income ever overshoots the measured curve by 40%, and names the four tuning knobs to turn, in order. (The bot is a perfect player, so a human curve runs below this one.)

12Details that are not details

Accessibility, performance, and the player’s time

When they are not playing

Offline earnings are capped on purpose: at most two hours of away time, at a quarter rate, based on a median of recent income rather than your best minute. The welcome-back card is a greeting and a reason to open the app — not a mechanism that makes playing feel pointless.

And it cannot be lost. During review I found that if the phone locked while the card was still open, the earnings were silently recomputed over and thrown away. Now the save timestamp is held until the player actually taps collect, and a resumed tab keeps the card. Someone who puts the phone down mid-greeting loses nothing.

Accessibility, specified alongside the mechanics

No state is communicated by colour alone — cold water gets frost hatching and a white dash on the rim, hot water grows steam wisps, affordable steps get a solid outline and unaffordable ones a dashed one, the upgrade sheet uses lock and check glyphs. “Shake & flash” can be turned off in one switch. Shake is bounded anyway at 8 px decaying within 0.3 s, and the frame freezes are capped at 150 ms per second and never freeze the joystick, so the game never stops answering your thumb even while the world holds still. Text is rationed to three words on screen, and speech bubbles carry one icon and no text at all, so nothing needs translating.

Performance as an art brief

A 12 ms frame budget and a 2 ms simulation budget shaped the visual language directly. No blurs, no filters, no gradients built per frame — every glow is one pre-rendered sprite blitted with varying alpha, capped at eight, which is why the night scenes look like paper lanterns rather than bloom. The static world is one cached image. Depth is a single sort of at most 150 objects by their feet position, which is exactly why the projection is flat with painted thickness bands instead of a real camera.

13Scope

What shipped

Scope was tiered on day one — Core, Plus, Stretch — and the tiers held. Build 1.2.0 contains all of Core and all of Plus: three baths with independent upgrade tracks, the Trail with four capacity tiers, the shared combo window, Heat with chaining Steam Rushes, the yuzu grove and golden baths, a snack stall with a food-order chain, two hireable helpers, cable cars with four size tiers plus duck flocks and Golden Cars, fourteen unlocks, Lantern Night, the bridge finale, save with offline earnings, and the full simulation harness.

Deliberately left for later: a crow micro-event, a dash move, and a second zone up the mountain. All specified, none built — the finale already lands at 25 minutes, and adding to the middle would have thinned it.

14Takeaways

What I learned

Specify the feel, not just the layout

The spec that made this buildable states that guests join 0.12 s apart, that the screen bumps 4 px on a three-chain and 8 px on a five, that the arrow sits 46 px above the player and bobs at 3 Hz. Those numbers are the design. Leaving them to “implementation detail” leaves the product to chance.

Testability is a design property

Making the guidance system a pure function of game state — rules with no hidden state — was originally a clarity decision. It is what made the game automatically playable, which is what found both failures. Designs that can be explained as rules can be verified as rules.

Constraints are faster than references

“No asset files” sounds like a limitation and behaved like a brief. It produced the silhouette test, the thickness-band depth language and a palette with actual grammar — none of which I would have reached by collecting screenshots of games I liked.

Measure before you tune

My hand-built economy was wrong in magnitude and right in shape. Adjusting payouts to match my own arithmetic would have broken pacing that was already correct. The instinct to fix is much stronger than the instinct to measure, and it is usually wrong.

What this project does not have

  • No session on a physical phone yet — verified by the simulator and in a mobile-sized browser. That 20-minute session on real glass is the next step, because feel on glass is the one thing neither the spec nor the simulator can report.
  • No human playtesters, so no retention, session-length or completion data. Every figure on this page is about the design process.
  • Not published. No store build, no downloads, no users. It runs from a file.

Capy Springs runs from a single HTML file with no network, no accounts and no analytics. Nothing about the player leaves their device. Every screenshot on this page is a capture from the running build at a chosen moment, not a mockup.
